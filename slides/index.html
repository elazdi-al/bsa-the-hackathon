One idea carries the design. The wallet key is split 2-of-3 with Shamir secret sharing: Share A on the phone, sealed so only Face ID opens it, B at Swissquote, C at an independent service. Any two rebuild the key on the phone, which keeps it in memory for three hours at most. The key alone moves nothing: the smart account also checks a passkey signature on every payment, so Swissquote and the independent service together still cannot move funds. Share C also has a second job, exit mode: if Swissquote is gone, it lets the client leave without it. We come back to that at the end of the story.

Before Swissquote sponsors a payment, its compliance engine decodes the call, checks the allowlist and limits, screens every address and simulates the balance changes. It signs a decision with a plain-language reading into a hash-chained audit log, and the phone shows that reading before Face ID, as here. Last, the brief asks what happens if Swissquote's services are gone for good. After 30 days without Swissquote, the independent service opens exit mode: when the client's passkey approves a payment, it releases Share C for that payment. The phone rebuilds the key from A and C, signs with Face ID and the key, and submits straight to the chain. With no Swissquote paymaster, the client pays the fee, a few cents of ETH. Now, why this design holds up. Of the five common models, custody is simple but it is the bank's wallet, and seed phrases or split paper backups (SLIP-39 is the standard for splitting a seed phrase) leave the client alone with paper. In a 2-of-3 MPC wallet, where the parties compute each signature together, any two holders can sign without the client, and a passkey-only wallet is only as safe as the cloud account that syncs the passkey. Ours keeps the bank out of spending even with a partner, needs no seed phrase, only a key file at home, and turns a stolen backup into a 48-hour recovery the client can cancel.

No server can move funds, alone or together: payments need the passkey, and recovery needs the Recovery Key plus 48 hours anyone can see. Lock-out is another matter. Swissquote alone can lock a client out by withholding Share B, refusing sponsorship or cancelling recoveries; exit mode helps only once it has been offline for 30 days. Swissquote holding a stolen Recovery Key could take the wallet after 48 hours if the client misses it, because both cancel paths in the app run through Swissquote. The main residual risk is a thief who can unlock the phone: they can pay within the limits until the client reports it and Swissquote locks it, and anything over 1,000 USDC waits 24 hours. A malicious app build is out of the protocol's scope; production needs a native app, reproducible builds and audits.

Architecture

Solid parts run for real in the demo. Dashed parts are mocked.

Four parts. The phone holds the passkey and Share A and rebuilds the key in memory. Swissquote screens every payment, sponsors and submits every operation and holds Share B. The independent service is a real separate process with its own database, holding Share C, which the phone sends it directly; only the company behind it is a stand-in. The chain enforces the passkey and the 48-hour delay; its EntryPoint is the standard contract that checks and runs every account operation. Dashed parts are mocked: the Swissquote sign-in, the KYC record and ID check, the sanctions and simulation data, and the vaults, which seal shares in software here and would be HSMs in production.

Assistance, not custody. Ownership signed at creation.

Our reading of Swiss law, for counsel to confirm.

Custody

Swissquote can’t move funds, even with the independent service. It can withhold help and cancel a recovery, which we argue is assistance.

Travel Rule

At creation the wallet key signs a statement naming the client. Automatic ownership evidence, for FINMA and counsel to accept.

Sending institution

The client signs; the account sends. Swissquote screens, sponsors and submits, but never holds or orders funds. We argue it isn’t the sender.

Custody turns on who can move the funds: Swissquote can't, alone or with the independent service; it can only withhold help or cancel a recovery, which we argue is assistance, still under AML rules, hence the screening. For the Travel Rule, the wallet is created inside the signed-in app and its key signs a statement naming the client, so ownership evidence exists from the first second, without a test transfer. When the client pays a third party, the client signs and the account sends, so we argue Swissquote is not the sending institution, an open question for counsel.
