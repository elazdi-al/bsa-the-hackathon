Recover after losing a phone.

Keep the same wallet.

The smart account is a contract, with an address that stays the same when its signer and passkey change. The Wallet Key is a signing key, not the account itself. We explain the design choices and then the setup and recovery steps. This is a prototype, with mocked bank identity verification.

Wallet Key+Passkey

The account verifies both signatures.

Normal mode checks the Wallet Key ECDSA signature and a WebAuthn assertion from the registered passkey over the same UserOperation hash. The phone verifies the operation before signing. On a cache miss, Swissquote verifies the assertion before releasing B. The key can remain in memory for at most three hours. The backend submits through EntryPoint v0.7 using a paymaster for sponsorship; there is no separate external bundler. B+C alone cannot satisfy the passkey check.

the old passkey.

A recovery request alone is not proof.

Allowing B+C alone to replace the signer and passkey would bypass the payment protection. An impostor requesting recovery presents the same authorization problem. We cannot require the old passkey in the lost-device case, so we introduce a separate Recovery Key.

Use a separate Recovery Key.

Create it during setup. Save it offline.

The Recovery Key is independent of Share C. Its public address is registered with the account and share holders; the private key stays with the user, normally saved away from the phone. A Wallet Key signature binds the Recovery Key to the shares. Both services verify recovery authorization, and the account verifies the two signatures. Identity verification is mocked in this build, so the secret signature is an essential part of the demonstrated protection. It is not another Shamir share. The Wallet Key signs its binding to the holders, and its public address is registered with the smart account.

After checking the Recovery Key signature, it releases old B and a five-minute signed approval ticket. The Recovery Key could be stolen.

A thief could request recovery.

Possession of the secret can authorize an assisted recovery attempt when the services are available. The Recovery Key alone cannot directly spend funds, because the old Wallet Key signature and service assistance are still needed. The waiting period provides a chance to detect and cancel fraudulent recovery, not a guarantee of prevention.

Recovery waits before taking effect.

48 hours

Cancel from the old phone or through Swissquote.

The contract sets its deadline to block.timestamp plus 48 hours. Changing the browser clock does not shorten it. The old phone cancels using normal authorization. Swissquote can cancel directly as guardian; its app endpoint requires a Recovery Key-signed request. A second recovery initiation is rejected while one is pending. Cancellation remains possible after the deadline if finalization has not happened. Effective detection and notification are needed in production; production alerts are not implemented.

Replace the signer and passkey.

Same wallet address. No assets moved.

Anyone can call finalization after the deadline, but only the recorded replacement is installed. The new phone uses its new A and Swissquote's new B for normal signing. The same account continues to hold the assets; ordinary transactions during the wait can still change its balances. Both services must remain available for the implemented recovery flow. Losing the Recovery Key as well removes this recovery authorization path.
